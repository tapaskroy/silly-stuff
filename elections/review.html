2. Where we went wrong

33 favorites lost: 2 in the Senate, 28 in the House, 3 for governor. Every House upset and both Senate upsets went to the Democrat. Upsets were expected, about 25 of them from our own odds, but they should have broken both ways. When 32 of 33 break the same way, the misses are one error, not 33: the whole forecast sat too far to the right. Each upset is written up in the appendix.

Senate: 2 upsets in 34 called races

Both misses were in the Deep South and both went to Democrats. No forecaster, rater or market we found favored Scott Colom in Mississippi, and only one little-known model favored Annie Andrews in South Carolina, so these were shared misses. South Carolina is the one we should have come closer on: 18 polls averaged Graham +0.9, but our fundamentals prior (Graham +7.7) pulled the forecast to Graham +1.5. Mississippi had only two polls, both showing Hyde-Smith ahead; reporting credits a surge in Black turnout that no poll captured.

Three things stand out:

  • We discounted the polls that were right. Partisan polls are shifted 4.5 points against their sponsor and given half weight. This year Democratic internal polls (Impact Research, GBAO, PPP for House Majority PAC, Hart) were among the most accurate in the field and were the polls most likely to show the upset winners ahead. Removing the shift alone fixes 6 House upsets and Nevada governor and cuts the House Brier score from 0.046 to 0.039. One cycle is not proof: the shift exists because internals usually do overstate their side.
  • The 1-point shading pointed the wrong way, and on its own fixes only 2 races. With both changes the House goes from 377 to 383 right and close races from 18 to 24 of 36.
  • Even perfect knowledge of the national vote leaves an average error of +2.2 in the House, and no rule change rescues Mississippi, South Carolina, TX-9, NJ-2 or the North Carolina seats. Those were not misreadings of the polls. The electorate itself was different from the one the fundamentals assumed.

What no rule would have caught

  • Who turned out. Reporting on the Deep South upsets (Mississippi, South Carolina, AL-2) and North Carolina credits a surge in Black turnout; in Mississippi the Black share of the electorate rose from about 30% to 33%, and on the 2024 electorate Hyde-Smith would have won. Our House prior is built from 2024 presidential margins, which bakes in the 2024 electorate.
  • Latino voters in South Texas swung back. The 2025 Texas map assumed Trump's 2024 Latino gains would hold. Voter surveys put Latino voters around 65–70% Democratic this year; all four border-area seats flipped and the Texas governor's race was, by some accounts, the largest statewide polling miss in the state's history.
  • Candidates and campaigns. Several Republican nominees were weak (Darline Graham, appointed after Lindsey Graham's death; Brandon Herrera in TX-23), and some Democrats won while badly outspent (NC-14 on about $56,000). Our House model has no candidate-quality term.
  • The other direction. Vermont's Phil Scott is a reminder that a single, systematically wrong pollster can sink a thinly polled race.

Lessons

  1. A model is only as good as its national number. Most of the House miss traces to the generic ballot. Shading it by a historical average added error this year; the honest version is to keep the uncertainty and drop the directional tilt.
  2. Correlated error has to show up in the race odds, not just the seat totals. The model simulates a national error, but it called 22 close races for Republicans and they broke 18–4 the other way. When most close races lean one way, a single national error flips them together, and the overview should say so.
  3. Treat partisan polls by track record, not by rule. A fixed 4.5-point penalty for every internal poll threw away the best signal in the races that decided the House.
  4. The prior should yield faster to polls. South Carolina had 18 polls and the prior still moved the margin 0.6 points; with a lighter prior the House would have caught MT-1 and the Senate odds would have been less confident.
  5. New maps need wider error. Redrawn seats are scored on 2024 margins from a different electorate. Texas and North Carolina, both on 2025 mid-decade maps, had the largest misses among states with many competitive seats.
  6. Calling every race is fine, but the scorecard must be honest about it. "Most races right" mostly reflects not using a toss-up category. Report the close-race record and the Brier score alongside it.

5. Summary, and what to change for 2028

Summary. The forecast called the Senate about as well as anyone and favored the right party for control of both chambers. It was slightly behind the field on governors and clearly behind every other model and market in the House. It underestimated Democrats almost everywhere, by 6.7 points in the average House race. Most of that was the national environment: we started from a generic-ballot number that was already low and lowered it further. On top of that, the rules for partisan polls threw away the information that pointed to the upsets, and a fundamentals prior built on 2024 margins could not see a changed electorate. Upsets themselves were expected; 33 of them, all but one in one direction, were not.

Changes for 2028 (rule-based, set before any 2028 polls, and tested on 2018–2026 before use):

  1. National environment without a directional tilt. Use the polling average at face value, and widen the national error instead of shading it. For a presidential year, the national popular-vote polls and the state polls should be tied together explicitly, so a state that moves pulls its neighbors.
  2. Pollster-specific house effects. Replace the flat partisan-sponsor penalty with each firm's measured bias from past cycles, shrunk toward zero for firms with little history. Track internal polls by sponsor and firm; this year that would have counted Impact and GBAO at close to face value.
  3. A lighter prior where polls are plentiful, and an explicit turnout-composition term for the fundamentals (who voted in 2024 versus who is registered and voting early), so a South Carolina or Mississippi can move before election night.
  4. Extra error for redrawn districts and for groups that swung hard last time (Latino voters in South Texas and Florida; rural Black turnout in the Deep South), applied by rule, not by hand.
  5. Publish both directions of polling miss on the overview (D+2, D+3 as well as R+2, R+3), and show how many close races lean each way.
  6. Score the forecast honestly: alongside "races right," publish the close-race record, Brier score and calibration, and compare against Sabato, the markets and the leading models on the same races.
  7. For the presidency itself: the Electoral College hinges on 7–8 states, so the state-to-state error correlation matters more than anything here. Backtest it on 2016, 2020 and 2024, when polls underestimated Republicans, and on 2026, when they underestimated Democrats; a model that handles both directions without a tilt is the target.

These are proposals for the 2028 model, not changes to the 2026 forecast, which stays frozen as published.
