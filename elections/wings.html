2026 midterms: Overview · Senate · House · Governors · Scorecard

The party wings of the new Congress

Every member-elect of the incoming House and Senate, sorted into four groups: two per party, by their votes and stated policies. Then the incoming Congress is compared with the outgoing one to see which wings gained seats and which lost them. Based on AP race calls through Nov 14, 2026: 425 of 435 House seats and 99 of 100 Senate seats. The rest are still being counted.

The four groups

Progressive Democrats

90 House14 Senate+18 seats vs. the outgoing Congress

The party's left. Progressives want a large expansion of public programs: Medicare for All, Green New Deal-scale climate spending, tuition-free college, higher taxes on the wealthy and on corporations. Most belong to the Congressional Progressive Caucus, and their backers include Bernie Sanders, Justice Democrats, the Working Families Party, Our Revolution and Sunrise. On Israel they favor conditioning or cutting U.S. military aid.

How a member lands here: a left economic program, and no vote to keep military aid to Israel. In the House the Israel test is the July 15, 2026 vote on the Massie amendment to strip $3.3 billion in Israel military aid; in the Senate, the April 15, 2026 vote on S.J.Res. 32 to block an arms sale to Israel.

Examples: Bernie Sanders, Elizabeth Warren, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Greg Casar; new: Abdul El-Sayed, Peggy Flanagan, Brad Lander.

Mainstream Democrats

166 House41 Senate+33 seats vs. the outgoing Congress

Everyone else in the Democratic caucus, from leadership-aligned liberals to the New Democrat Coalition and Blue Dog moderates. They tend to build on existing programs (the Affordable Care Act, targeted tax credits) rather than replace them, are more open to business and fiscal caution, and back security aid to Israel. Under this page's rule, members with a left economic record who voted to keep Israel aid are counted here.

How a member lands here: everyone else in the Democratic caucus: members without a left economic program, and members with one who voted to keep Israel aid.

Examples: Hakeem Jeffries, Mark Warner, Rosa DeLauro, Ritchie Torres; new: Roy Cooper, Sherrod Brown, Zack Mullock.

Nationalist Republicans

85 House17 Senate-15 seats vs. the outgoing Congress

The America First wing. These members are skeptical of foreign aid and long-term commitments abroad, and want immigration cut beyond the party's baseline: ending birthright citizenship, cutting legal immigration, mass deportation. They back tariffs and put cultural and religious issues at the center of their platforms, and many objected to certifying the 2020 election. This isn't the same thing as the Freedom Caucus, whose hardliners fight mostly over spending and House procedure.

How a member lands here: a vote to bar Ukraine aid: in the House the July 2025 Greene amendment, and in the Senate the April 2024 Ukraine aid package. Members whose votes are mixed, and newcomers, are placed on their platforms and backers. A Trump endorsement alone doesn't decide it.

Examples: Josh Hawley, Mike Lee, Ted Cruz, Andy Biggs (outgoing); new: Barry Moore, Harriet Hageman (Senate), Anthony Constantino.

Mainstream Republicans

84 House27 Senate-34 seats vs. the outgoing Congress

Traditional conservatives. They want tax cuts and deregulation, and back a strong defense, NATO and aid to allies, including the 2024 Ukraine aid. They are more aligned with business and more open to trade, and include the Republican Main Street Caucus and the Problem Solvers. They are still conservative on immigration; what separates them from the Nationalists is mostly foreign policy, trade and tone.

How a member lands here: a vote for the 2024 Ukraine aid without a vote to bar it later. Members with no record are placed on their platforms and backers.

Examples: John Thune, Mike Rounds, Shelley Moore Capito, Brian Fitzpatrick (outgoing); new: Kurt Alme, Andy Barr (Senate).

Who gained and who lost

  • Mainstream Democrats gained the most: +33 seats (+30 House, +3 Senate). The Democrats who flipped Republican seats were mostly mainstream candidates: of the 41 new House Democrats who took a seat a Republican held, 34 are Mainstream, from Zack Mullock (NJ-2) to Pia Dandiya (FL-22). In the Senate, so are Roy Cooper (North Carolina), Sherrod Brown (Ohio), Josh Turek (Iowa), Adam Hamilton (Kansas), Annie Andrews (South Carolina) and Scott Colom (Mississippi). Mainstream Democrats are now the largest bloc in the House, 166 of 425 called seats.
  • Mainstream Republicans lost the most: -34 seats (-25 House, -9 Senate). They held more of the competitive seats, so the wave hit them hardest: of the 33 House Republicans who lost their general elections, 24 were Mainstream and 9 Nationalist. In the Senate, Collins, Tillis, Cornyn, Ernst, Cassidy and Hyde-Smith are all leaving; their successors are Democrats or Nationalist Republicans.
  • Progressive Democrats grew too: +18 seats (+13 House, +5 Senate). In the House, 15 of the 24 new progressives succeeded other Democrats, mostly in safe seats, and 7 won Republican-held seats. The five new progressive senators won open Democratic seats (Michigan, Minnesota, Illinois) or Republican-held ones (Maine, Texas). In the Senate they grew fastest, from 9 to 14, and are now 25% of the Democratic caucus, up from 19%. In the House their share of the caucus barely moved (36% to 35%).
  • Nationalist Republicans lost seats but gained ground inside their party: -15 seats overall (-16 House, +1 Senate). Because Mainstream Republicans lost more, Nationalists are now a bigger share of a smaller conference: 50% of House Republicans (from 48%) and 39% of Senate Republicans (from 31%).

Head to head: who did better against whom

Every competitive race, meaning one the forecast didn't rate Safe for either party (Likely, Lean, Tilt or Toss-up), with both nominees sorted into wings: 139 called House races and 24 Senate races. Win counts mostly reflect how red or blue the seats were, since Progressive-vs-Nationalist races were fought on much redder ground. So the yardstick here is how far the Democrat beat the forecast margin, the pre-election expectation for that seat, which already counts its lean, incumbency and polls. A bigger number means the Democrat did better than expected, so the Republican did worse.

  • The wave lifted every matchup. On average, House Democrats beat the forecast by +7.2 points, and every matchup landed between +5.2 and +8.2. Next to a 7-point wave, the wings mattered a little, not a lot.
  • Mainstream Democrats against Nationalist Republicans was the best matchup for Democrats (+8.1), and Progressive Democrats against Nationalist Republicans the weakest (+5.2, only 9 races). Against the same Mainstream Democrats, Nationalist Republicans fell 1.3 points further behind the forecast than Mainstream Republicans did. Overall, Democrats beat the forecast by +7.5 against Nationalists and +7.0 against Mainstream Republicans.
  • Progressive Democrats did about as well as Mainstream ones against Mainstream Republicans (+8.2 vs. +6.8), but worse against Nationalists (+5.2 vs. +8.1). The Progressive rows rest on few races, 13 and 9, so one race can swing them. CA-22 (Randy Villegas over David Valadao, only 94% counted) alone lifts the Progressive-vs-Mainstream average by 1.7 points.
  • How sure is this? Not very. The gap between the best and worst matchups is about 3.1 points, and the race-to-race spread is about 5 points. Only the Mainstream-vs-Nationalist edge over Mainstream-vs-Mainstream rests on enough races to mean much (38 and 79), and even it is about 1.3 standard errors: suggestive, not proven.

The upsets (31)

The 31 races the favorite lost: 29 in the House plus the Senate races in Mississippi and South Carolina. Every one was a Democrat beating a favored Republican.

  • The pattern repeats, more sharply. Upsets with a Mainstream Democrat against a Nationalist Republican were the biggest misses (+11.8 vs. the forecast). Upsets where both nominees were Mainstream missed by +8.6. Nationalist Republicans who lost as favorites tended to lose by a lot, while Mainstream ones tended to lose narrowly.
  • But Nationalists weren't upset more often. Of the House races where the Republican was favored, Democrats pulled the upset in 10 of 31 Mainstream-vs-Nationalist races and 15 of 46 Mainstream-vs-Mainstream races, about a third each. Progressives pulled 3 of 9 against Mainstream Republicans and 1 of 9 against Nationalists.
  • Most upset winners were Mainstream Democrats: 27 of 31, mostly because they were the nominees in most of the Republican-leaning seats in play.

Method and caveats

  • Two wings per party, on purpose. This scheme is simple. It folds moderates and leadership loyalists into "Mainstream", and it puts libertarians like Rand Paul with the Nationalists because of their foreign-aid votes. Both parties have more factions than this.
  • Votes decide where they can. For sitting members, the wing comes from recorded roll calls: the Israel votes above for Democrats, the Ukraine votes above for Republicans (sources: clerk.house.gov, and Voteview for the Senate). Where the votes don't settle it (for example, a Republican who opposed the 2024 aid but not the 2025 ban), the member is placed on other policies: immigration, trade, caucus memberships, 2020 election objections.
  • Newcomers are provisional. Members with no voting record are placed on their platforms, endorsements and backers; 87 of the placements are marked low confidence. Caucuses form in December and January, and some newcomers will vote differently than they campaigned.
  • The Israel rule is a choice, and it matters in the House. Without it, the House would have more Progressive Democrats, because a number of members with left economic records voted to keep the Israel aid in July 2026. In the Senate, it moves only one senator (Richard Blumenthal).
  • Research by AI agents, checked against the roll calls. Each member was researched from public sources, and the wing was then recomputed from the recorded votes with one rule for everyone. Caucus memberships and endorsements came partly from Wikipedia and may contain errors. Three California seats (CA-4, CA-7 and CA-40) were same-party races, and those entries show the current leader.
